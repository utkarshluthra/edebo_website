Rhinoplasty Consultations With 3D Imaging: Plan and Explain

How plastic surgeons use 3D imaging in rhinoplasty and non-surgical nose consultations to analyse the profile, document asymmetry and align expectations.

Rhinoplasty consultations carry a unique challenge: the patient sees their nose mostly from the front, in a mirror, while much of the surgical plan is about the profile and three-dimensional shape. 3D imaging gives surgeon and patient a shared, accurate view of the nose from every angle.

Why the nose is hard to discuss with photos

  • Perspective distortion: close-up phone selfies make the nose look larger than it is, which shapes many patients' self-image. See why 2D before-and-after photos mislead.
  • Hidden angles: the base view (from below) and three-quarter views are rarely captured well.
  • Asymmetry: deviations and tip asymmetry are hard to see consistently in 2D.

What 3D imaging shows

On a 3D model, the surgeon can rotate freely and assess:

  • Dorsal profile: humps, saddle shape, overall line.
  • Tip projection and rotation in relation to the face.
  • Nasolabial angle between the columella and upper lip.
  • Nasal width at the base and bridge.
  • Deviation and asymmetry, using mirroring across the facial midline. See facial symmetry analysis.
  • Relationship to chin and lips: many patients benefit from understanding how chin projection affects the perceived size of the nose. See jawline and chin contouring.

A consultation structure

  1. Scan the patient at the start of the consultation, neutral expression, head level.
  2. Let the patient look at their own nose in 3D, especially in profile and base view. Many see it differently from how they imagined.
  3. Ask the patient to point to what they want to change, on the model.
  4. Explain the anatomy and what is realistic, using measurements where helpful.
  5. Annotate the agreed goals on the model and record them in the notes.
  6. Document asymmetry that will or will not be corrected.

Imaging vs simulation

It is worth being clear with patients about the difference:

  • Imaging records what is there now. It is a measurement.
  • Simulation shows one possible outcome. It is an illustration.

If you use simulation, label it clearly and explain that tissue healing varies. Many surgeons prefer to agree goals on the real model and avoid showing a simulated "after" that patients may treat as a promise. Our guide on managing patient expectations covers this.

Post-operative documentation

Nose surgery involves swelling that resolves over a long period, especially at the tip. Scheduled 3D scans through recovery give the patient reassurance that change is continuing and give the surgeon a measured record of the result. Compare each scan with baseline in profile and base views.

Non-surgical rhinoplasty

For filler-based profile changes, the same baseline and review approach applies. A colour map shows exactly where the profile changed. See dermal filler volume measurement.

Measurements surgeons commonly record

A consistent set of measurements makes pre- and post-operative comparison meaningful. Many surgeons record some version of the following on the 3D model:

Measurement Why it matters
Nasal length Relationship to facial thirds
Tip projection How far the tip sits from the face
Nasolabial angle Tip rotation relative to the upper lip
Nasofrontal angle Depth and position of the radix
Alar base width Width relative to intercanthal distance
Dorsal deviation Straightness relative to the facial midline

The value of these numbers lies in consistency. Measured on the same landmarks at baseline and at each recovery milestone, they show how the result is maturing, especially at the tip, where swelling can persist for many months.

Talking about change in the patient's terms

Patients rarely think in angles. They think in phrases like "the bump", "it looks wide from the front" or "it droops when I smile". Translate each measurement back into those terms: "your nasal tip sits lower than you'd like; that's this angle here, and the plan is to lift it slightly." When the patient's own words, the model and the measurement all line up, the consultation feels clear rather than technical.

Record those phrases in the notes too. At follow-up, revisiting the patient's original words alongside the measured change is one of the most effective ways to show that the agreed goals were met. See measuring aesthetic results in millimetres.

A note on scope

3D imaging is a measurement and visualisation tool. It supports the surgeon's assessment of external shape; it does not assess internal nasal structures or airway function, which need clinical examination.

Frequently asked questions

How does 3D imaging help in rhinoplasty consultations?

It lets the surgeon and patient view the nose from every angle on the patient's own face, measure profile and proportions, document asymmetry and discuss the plan with a shared reference.

Is 3D imaging the same as rhinoplasty simulation?

No. Imaging records the patient's current nose. Simulation predicts a possible result. If a simulation is used, it should be clearly presented as an illustration, not a promise.

Can 3D imaging be used for non-surgical nose treatments?

Yes. For non-surgical profile changes with filler, a baseline and review scan show the measured change in the nasal profile.
