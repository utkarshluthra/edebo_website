Clinical Photography Setup for a Small Clinic on a Budget

How a small dermatology or aesthetic clinic in India can set up consistent clinical photography and 3D capture in one room without expensive equipment.

You do not need a studio to take consistent clinical images. A small clinic can build a reliable setup in one corner of a consult room, for far less than the cost of one imaging rig. This guide covers what to buy, how to arrange it and how to keep it consistent.

What you need

Item Purpose Notes
Clinic-owned smartphone Photos and 3D scans Keep it in the clinic; do not use staff phones
Plain matte backdrop Removes visual clutter Dark blue, black or neutral grey-violet; no gloss
Two soft LED panels Even light Daylight-balanced, on stands at face height
Floor marks Fixed distance Tape marks for stool and photographer
Stool without a high back Lets you move around the head Swivel stools help
Tripod or phone stand Optional, for 2D consistency Not used during 3D capture
Hair bands, wipes, blotting paper Patient prep Keep a small tray ready
Secure storage Linked to patient record Not a gallery app or WhatsApp

Arranging the space

  1. Choose a wall without a window. Daylight changes through the day and backlight ruins both photos and 3D scans.
  2. Hang the backdrop behind where the patient will sit.
  3. Place the stool about a metre in front of the backdrop to avoid shadows on it.
  4. Set the two lights at roughly 45 degrees either side of the patient, at face height, angled slightly inward.
  5. Mark the floor for the stool position and for the photographer's standing position for 2D shots.
  6. Leave a clear circle around the stool so you can walk an arc for 3D capture.

If you cannot dedicate a wall, a roll-down backdrop and two foldable lights can be set up in minutes.

Lighting basics

The aim is soft, even light that shows the face as it is:

  • Turn off overhead tube lights if they create shadows under the eyes and nose.
  • Keep both lights at the same brightness and colour temperature.
  • Check for shine on the forehead and nose; if it appears, diffuse the lights or blot the skin.

Our 3D scan lighting and capture tips go into more detail, and the same rules apply to photography.

Making it consistent

Equipment does not create consistency; habits do.

  • Write a one-page protocol and pin it on the wall. Use our standardised before-and-after protocol as a base.
  • Train every staff member who takes images, and check their first ten patients.
  • Do a monthly test shot of a staff volunteer to spot drift in lighting or positioning.

Adding 3D without adding equipment

This same corner works for phone-based 3D capture. The patient sits on the stool, the clinician walks a slow arc around them, and the model is ready in seconds. No extra hardware or room is needed, which is why phone-based systems suit small clinics. Compare options in 3D imaging rig vs smartphone.

Storage and privacy

The weakest point in most small clinics is not the camera but where images end up. Personal phones, shared drives and messaging apps all create risk under the DPDP Act. Read stop storing patient photos on staff phones and set up a single, secure home for every image.

What it costs

A backdrop, two LED panels, stands and a stool can usually be put together for a modest one-time spend. A phone-based 3D subscription such as Edebo starts at ₹1,999 a month. Compared with a dedicated imaging room, that is a small fraction of the cost, as we show in the cost of 3D imaging for clinics in India.

Frequently asked questions

What equipment do I need for clinical photography in a small clinic?

A clinic-owned smartphone or camera, a plain matte backdrop, two soft LED lights, floor markings for fixed distance, a stool without a high back and a secure place to store images.

Can I use the same room for photos and 3D scans?

Yes. The same even, diffuse lighting that suits standard photos also suits phone-based 3D scanning. Leave space to walk around the patient's stool.

Is a DSLR better than a phone for clinic photos?

A DSLR with a fixed lens gives excellent 2D images, but a modern phone is adequate for most clinical documentation, easier to standardise and can also run 3D capture.
