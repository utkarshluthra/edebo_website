Measuring Aesthetic Results in Millimetres, Not Opinions

How 3D measurement turns aesthetic outcomes into numbers: volume, projection, symmetry and contour change between visits, and how to present them to patients.

Edebo Team7 July 20263 min read

  3. Presenting results to patients

Aesthetic medicine has always had a measurement problem. The treatment is precise, measured in millilitres, units and joules, but the result is judged by eye, from photos, often weeks later, by a patient who no longer remembers their starting point. 3D imaging lets clinics close that loop with numbers.

What can be measured

Once you have two aligned 3D scans, Day 0 and a follow-up, several outcomes become measurable.

Volume change

Software calculates the volume between the two surfaces over a region, such as the midface or the lips. This is the most direct way to show what a filler or fat graft achieved, and how it has settled. See dermal filler volume measurement.

Remember the evidence: in published studies, phone-based volume agreement is looser than distance agreement, so read very small volume changes with care. Details in how accurate is smartphone 3D face scanning.

Projection and contour

How far a feature stands out: chin, cheekbone, nasal tip, lips. Useful for jawline and chin contouring and rhinoplasty consultations.

Distances and angles

Lip height, intercanthal width, nasolabial angle, hairline position. Distances along the skin surface can matter as much as straight-line distances.

Symmetry

Mirroring one side of the face onto the other shows where and how much the two sides differ, before and after treatment. See facial symmetry analysis.

Skin features

Pigmentation, redness, texture and pores can be scored consistently from each scan, which turns a skin programme into a trend line rather than a debate. See pigmentation and melasma tracking.

How alignment works

Comparison only works if the two scans are lined up correctly. Software aligns them on reference regions that should not change with treatment, typically the forehead, nose bridge and around the eyes. Once aligned, any remaining difference in the treated area reflects real change, not head position.

This is the key advantage over 2D photos, where pose, distance and lighting differences can create or hide a result. We explain those distortions in why 2D before-and-after photos mislead.

Presenting results to patients

Numbers persuade only when they are understood. A simple structure:

  1. Show the two faces side by side in 3D, rotating to the angle that shows the area best.
  2. Turn on the colour map, explaining that one colour means added volume and the other means reduced volume.
  3. Give one or two key numbers, such as "about 1 ml of volume restored in the left midface" or "chin projection forward by 3 mm".
  4. Put it in context: what was planned, what is typical, what happens next.
  5. Avoid overclaiming. Measurements describe shape; they do not diagnose or guarantee future results.

Why measurement changes the business

  • Fewer disputes: when a patient feels "nothing changed", a measured colour map settles the conversation calmly.
  • More renewals: patients who see measured progress are more willing to continue a programme. See aesthetic clinic patient retention.
  • Better planning: knowing how much volume a patient retained at three months informs the next session's dose.
  • Better records: dated, consented, measured scans are stronger documentation than a folder of photos.

Building it into your workflow

Measurement is only as good as the baseline. Make the Day 0 scan non-negotiable for every volume or contour treatment, and book the follow-up scan at the time of treatment. Our follow-up tracking guide covers scheduling.

Frequently asked questions

How do you measure aesthetic treatment results objectively?

By aligning 3D scans from before and after treatment and measuring the difference: volume change, projection, distances and symmetry. Skin features can be tracked with consistent skin analysis scores.

What is a volume colour map?

A colour overlay on the 3D face that shows where the surface moved outward or inward between two scans, and by how much. It makes small volume changes visible to patients.

Should I show patients the measurements?

Yes, with context. Show the colour map and one or two key numbers, explain what is normal for the treatment, and avoid presenting the numbers as a diagnosis or a guarantee.
