Hair restoration is a long game. A patient who has a transplant today will not see the full result for many months, and by then they rarely remember exactly how their hairline looked before. That makes documentation unusually important, and unusually hard with standard photos.

## Why hairlines are hard to photograph

The hairline curves across the forehead and temples, and its appearance changes with:

- **head tilt**: tilting forward exposes more scalp,
- **lighting**: overhead light makes thinning look worse,
- **hair styling**: wet, combed back, or styled hair all look different,
- **camera height**: shooting from above versus at eye level changes the visible frontal hairline.

A standard set of 2D photos can capture this, but only with strict discipline at every visit. See [standardised before-and-after photos](/blog/standardised-before-after-photos-clinic/).

## What 3D adds for hair restoration

A 3D scan captures the forehead, temples, hairline and visible scalp shape in one pass. On the model, the surgeon can:

- view the hairline from the front, sides and above at once,
- **measure** the distance from landmarks such as the brow line to the frontal hairline,
- check hairline **symmetry** between left and right temples,
- **mark** the proposed new hairline and recipient zones directly on the model,
- estimate the **surface area** of zones to be treated.

On Edebo, annotation and markings on the face, hairline and body are available on Pro and Premium plans.

## A 3D workflow for hair transplant clinics

1. **Consultation scan**: hair dry and styled the way the patient normally wears it, plus a second scan with hair pulled back if needed.
2. **Planning on the model**: draw the proposed hairline, discuss height, shape and temple points with the patient, and record their agreement.
3. **Pre-operative scan** on the day of surgery, ideally after the hairline is marked, to record the final plan.
4. **Progress scans** at agreed intervals through the first year.
5. **Comparison** at each visit, showing the planned hairline against the current one.

## Hairline design considerations

Designing a hairline involves decisions that 3D imaging helps make visible:

- **Height**: where the frontal hairline sits relative to the brows and the overall face. A hairline set too low can look unnatural as the patient ages.
- **Shape**: rounded, straight or with a subtle central point, chosen to suit the face.
- **Temple points**: whether and how to rebuild the temporal angles, which strongly affect how natural the frame looks.
- **Symmetry**: small differences between sides look natural; large ones do not.
- **Future loss**: planning for how surrounding hair may change over the coming years.

Marking these on the 3D model, viewed from the front, both sides and above, lets the patient see the plan in context rather than as a line drawn on a mirror reflection. It also gives the surgical team a clear reference on the day of surgery.

## Consent for the hairline design

Hairline design is a decision the patient lives with permanently. Recording the agreed hairline on a 3D model, with the patient's documented approval, creates a clear record of what was planned. That protects both sides if there is ever a disagreement. See [patient consent for clinical photography](/blog/patient-consent-clinical-photography/) for imaging consent.

## What 3D imaging does not do

- It does **not count grafts** or measure hair density. That needs trichoscopy or close-up imaging.
- It does **not diagnose** the cause of hair loss. Clinical assessment does that.
- It records **shape and position**, which is exactly what hairline design needs.

## Why it matters for the business

Hair restoration patients often doubt progress in the slow early months. Showing them measured comparisons at each visit, against their own baseline, keeps them engaged with aftercare and more likely to recommend the clinic.
