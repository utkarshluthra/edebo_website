You do not need a studio to take consistent clinical images. A small clinic can build a reliable setup in one corner of a consult room, for far less than the cost of one imaging rig. This guide covers what to buy, how to arrange it and how to keep it consistent.

## What you need

| Item | Purpose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Clinic-owned smartphone | Photos and 3D scans | Keep it in the clinic; do not use staff phones |
| Plain matte backdrop | Removes visual clutter | Dark blue, black or neutral grey-violet; no gloss |
| Two soft LED panels | Even light | Daylight-balanced, on stands at face height |
| Floor marks | Fixed distance | Tape marks for stool and photographer |
| Stool without a high back | Lets you move around the head | Swivel stools help |
| Tripod or phone stand | Optional, for 2D consistency | Not used during 3D capture |
| Hair bands, wipes, blotting paper | Patient prep | Keep a small tray ready |
| Secure storage | Linked to patient record | Not a gallery app or WhatsApp |

## Arranging the space

1. **Choose a wall without a window.** Daylight changes through the day and backlight ruins both photos and 3D scans.
2. **Hang the backdrop** behind where the patient will sit.
3. **Place the stool** about a metre in front of the backdrop to avoid shadows on it.
4. **Set the two lights** at roughly 45 degrees either side of the patient, at face height, angled slightly inward.
5. **Mark the floor** for the stool position and for the photographer's standing position for 2D shots.
6. **Leave a clear circle** around the stool so you can walk an arc for 3D capture.

If you cannot dedicate a wall, a roll-down backdrop and two foldable lights can be set up in minutes.

## Lighting basics

The aim is soft, even light that shows the face as it is:

- Turn off overhead tube lights if they create shadows under the eyes and nose.
- Keep both lights at the same brightness and colour temperature.
- Check for shine on the forehead and nose; if it appears, diffuse the lights or blot the skin.

Our [3D scan lighting and capture tips](/blog/3d-scan-lighting-and-capture-tips/) go into more detail, and the same rules apply to photography.

## Making it consistent

Equipment does not create consistency; habits do.

- Write a one-page protocol and pin it on the wall. Use our [standardised before-and-after protocol](/blog/standardised-before-after-photos-clinic/) as a base.
- Train every staff member who takes images, and check their first ten patients.
- Do a monthly test shot of a staff volunteer to spot drift in lighting or positioning.

## Adding 3D without adding equipment

This same corner works for phone-based 3D capture. The patient sits on the stool, the clinician walks a slow arc around them, and the model is ready in seconds. No extra hardware or room is needed, which is why phone-based systems suit small clinics. Compare options in [3D imaging rig vs smartphone](/blog/3d-imaging-rig-vs-smartphone/).

## Storage and privacy

The weakest point in most small clinics is not the camera but where images end up. Personal phones, shared drives and messaging apps all create risk under the DPDP Act. Read [stop storing patient photos on staff phones](/blog/stop-storing-patient-photos-on-staff-phones/) and set up a single, secure home for every image.

## What it costs

A backdrop, two LED panels, stands and a stool can usually be put together for a modest one-time spend. A phone-based 3D subscription such as Edebo starts at ₹1,999 a month.
