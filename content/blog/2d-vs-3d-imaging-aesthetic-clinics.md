Every aesthetic clinic documents its work. The question is what that documentation can prove. Standard 2D photography and 3D imaging both have a place, but they answer different questions. This comparison will help you decide where each belongs in your workflow.

## The core difference: depth

A 2D photograph flattens the face onto a plane. You see outline and colour from one direction, but depth is gone. A 3D scan records the surface itself, so depth, curvature and volume are preserved.

That single difference drives everything else in the table below.

| Question | 2D photography | 3D imaging |
|---|---|---|
| Can I see the face from any angle? | Only the angles you shot | Yes, rotate freely |
| Can I measure volume? | No | Yes, between two scans |
| Can I measure distances in mm? | Only roughly, with a scale reference | Yes, on the model |
| Is comparison affected by pose and lighting? | Strongly | Much less, scans are aligned |
| Can I check left-right symmetry? | By eye | By mirroring and measuring |
| Is it good for colour and surface detail? | Yes | Yes, with texture mapping |
| Equipment needed | Camera, ideally a fixed setup | Rig, scanner or a phone |

## Where 2D photography still works well

2D photography is fast, familiar and cheap. It is well suited to:

- **Colour-led concerns** such as acne activity or a single pigmented lesion, viewed head-on.
- **Quick visual notes** during a procedure.
- **Marketing images**, with patient consent, where the goal is illustration rather than measurement.

To be useful even for these, 2D photos need discipline: same distance, same lens, same lighting, same head position. Our [standardised before-and-after photo guide](/blog/standardised-before-after-photos-clinic/) covers that protocol.

## Where 2D photography breaks down

The trouble starts when the result is a change in **shape**:

- A cheek filler adds a few millimetres of projection. From the front, that is almost invisible. From the side, it depends on exactly where the camera was.
- A patient who tilts their chin down by a few degrees looks like they have more submental fullness, which can wipe out a real result from a fat reduction course.
- Different light direction changes shadows, and shadows are how we perceive contour in a photo.

These are not edge cases. They happen in most clinics most days. We go deeper in [why 2D before-and-after photos mislead](/blog/why-2d-before-after-photos-mislead/).

## Where 3D imaging adds value

3D imaging earns its place wherever you need to answer "how much changed?":

- **Volume treatments**: fillers, fat grafting, and their follow-up.
- **Contour treatments**: jawline, chin, nose, and body contouring.
- **Symmetry work**: correcting asymmetry and documenting the baseline.
- **Long courses**: hair restoration, skin programmes and maintenance plans where progress is gradual and patients forget their starting point.

It also helps at the very start, during consultation. A patient who sees their own face in 3D, turned to show the area of concern, understands the plan faster. That has a direct effect on conversion; see [how to increase aesthetic consultation conversion](/blog/increase-aesthetic-consultation-conversion/).

## The honest limits of 3D

3D imaging is not a cure-all:

- It is a **measurement and visualisation tool**, not a diagnostic test.
- Results depend on capture conditions; poor light gives a poorer model. See [lighting and capture tips](/blog/3d-scan-lighting-and-capture-tips/).
- Very fine surface detail, such as individual fine lines, can be harder to resolve than in a high-resolution macro photo.
- Facial expression must be consistent between scans, just as with photos.

## A practical combined workflow

Most clinics that adopt 3D do not abandon 2D. A sensible split looks like this:

1. **Consultation**: 3D scan for the area of concern, shown to the patient.
2. **Treatment day**: quick 2D photos if useful for notes.
3. **Follow-up**: 3D scan aligned with Day 0, with measured change shown to the patient.
4. **Records**: both stored with dated, documented consent.
