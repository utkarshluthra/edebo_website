Skin analysis has become a common part of dermatology and aesthetic consultations, from dedicated analysis machines to app-based tools. Used well, it gives clinicians and patients a consistent way to describe the skin and track change. Used badly, it becomes a sales gimmick. Here is what it actually does.

## What skin analysis measures

Most tools score a set of **visible** features from a standardised capture:

| Feature | What it reflects | Typical treatment link |
|---|---|---|
| Pigmentation | Brown spots, uneven tone, melasma patches | Topicals, peels, lasers |
| Redness | Visible redness and vessels | Anti-redness regimens, vascular lasers |
| Texture | Surface roughness, scarring, unevenness | Resurfacing, microneedling |
| Pores | Visible pore size and density | Peels, energy treatments, skin care |

Some tools add wrinkles, oiliness or other features. Each score is a summary of what the camera sees under that capture's conditions.

## How it works

The software analyses colour and surface detail in the image or scan, identifies regions that match each feature, and converts them into a score or map. On a 3D scan, these maps can be laid over the face model so you can see where on the face each concern sits.

## Why consistency is everything

A skin analysis score is only comparable across visits if capture is consistent: same lighting, bare skin, no filters, similar head position. Change the light and the pigmentation score can change without any change in the skin. ## What skin analysis is good for

- **Baseline documentation** at the start of a programme.
- **Trend tracking** across sessions, which is more meaningful than any single score.
- **Patient education**: a map of redness or pigmentation helps patients understand their skin.
- **Programme planning**: deciding which concern to address first.
- **Retention**: patients who see a measured trend are more likely to continue. See [aesthetic clinic patient retention](/blog/aesthetic-clinic-patient-retention/).

## What it is not

- It is **not a diagnosis**. It does not identify disease or replace clinical examination.
- It is **not an absolute number**. Scores are best compared with the same patient's earlier scores under the same conditions, not across different devices or clinics.
- It is **not a sales script**. Using alarming scores to push products erodes trust quickly.

## Using skin analysis across a treatment course

Skin analysis is most valuable when it is planned into the whole course, not just the first visit:

1. **Consultation**: baseline scores and face map, used to agree priorities.
2. **Before each session**: a quick capture to confirm the skin is ready and to track the trend.
3. **Milestone reviews**: side-by-side comparison of maps and scores with the baseline.
4. **Maintenance**: periodic captures to catch relapse early, especially for pigmentation.

## How to present skin analysis to patients

1. Show the face map first, then the scores.
2. Explain each feature in one sentence.
3. Focus on the one or two concerns the patient cares about.
4. At follow-up, show the trend, with the same capture conditions noted.
5. Avoid comparing patients with "average" scores; compare them with themselves.
