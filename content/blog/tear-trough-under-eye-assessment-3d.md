The under-eye area is one of the most common concerns in aesthetic consultations and one of the hardest to document honestly. Patients describe "dark circles" or "tired eyes"; clinicians see a mix of hollowing, pigmentation, skin quality and fat pad changes. Photos rarely settle the question, because this area is defined by shadow.

## Why photos struggle here

The tear trough is a shallow groove where the lower eyelid meets the cheek. What we perceive as its depth is mostly shadow. Overhead light deepens it dramatically; frontal or ring light can almost erase it. A slight downward head tilt makes it look worse. That means a "before" and "after" can show improvement, or decline, purely through lighting. We cover these effects in [why 2D before-and-after photos mislead](/blog/why-2d-before-after-photos-mislead/).

## What 3D adds

A 3D scan records the actual **contour** of the lid-cheek junction rather than its shadow. That gives the clinician:

- a view of the hollow from any angle, including the oblique view where it is usually most visible,
- a way to compare left and right sides,
- a measured comparison between baseline and review, shown as a colour map,
- separation of **shape** (hollowing) from **colour** (pigmentation), when combined with skin analysis.

## Separating the causes

Dark circles are not one problem. Common contributors include:

| Contributor | What helps document it |
|---|---|
| Hollowing / volume loss | 3D contour and volume change |
| Pigmentation | Consistent skin analysis scores; see [pigmentation and melasma tracking](/blog/pigmentation-and-melasma-tracking/) |
| Visible vessels and redness | Redness scores and standardised photos |
| Fat pad prominence | 3D profile and oblique contour |
| Skin laxity and texture | Texture scores and close-up photos |

Clinical assessment decides the cause and the treatment. Imaging helps document each component so progress can be tracked honestly. Remember that imaging is a measurement and visualisation tool, not a diagnostic test.

## A 3D workflow for under-eye treatment

1. **Baseline scan** with a neutral expression, eyes open, looking straight ahead, under consistent lighting.
2. **Consultation**: show the oblique view of the model and explain what is contour and what is colour.
3. **Plan and annotate** the area on the model.
4. **Review scan** at the interval your protocol uses, often around two weeks for fillers.
5. **Compare**: show the contour change and, separately, any change in pigmentation or redness scores.

## Setting expectations

Under-eye treatments often produce subtle changes. A patient expecting dark circles to vanish may be disappointed even when the hollow has measurably improved, because pigmentation remains. Splitting the problem into contour and colour at the start, and showing both measures at follow-up, keeps the conversation honest. See [managing patient expectations](/blog/managing-patient-expectations-aesthetics/).

## Timing the review

The under-eye area is prone to swelling and bruising after injection, and lymphatic drainage here can be slow. A review scan taken too early can show a fuller contour than the settled result, which may lead to disappointment later or unnecessary concern. Agree a review interval that allows swelling to settle, and capture under the same conditions as baseline.

If a patient returns early with concerns, scan them anyway and record the interval. A series of scans over the settling period gives both patient and clinician reassurance that the contour is evolving as expected.

## Capture tips specific to this area

- Remove all eye makeup and concealer.
- Ask the patient to keep eyes open and relaxed, not squinting.
- Make sure the phone covers the oblique angles on both sides.
- Keep lighting identical between visits; this area is the most sensitive to light changes.
