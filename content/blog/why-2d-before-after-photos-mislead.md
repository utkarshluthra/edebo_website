Most clinicians have seen it: a patient comes for a follow-up, you compare photos, and the result looks smaller than you remember, or bigger than it should. Sometimes the treatment was the variable. Often it was the camera.

Two-dimensional photos are useful, but they are surprisingly easy to distort, even without any intent to mislead. Understanding how helps clinics show results honestly and helps patients trust what they see.

## 1. Camera distance distorts proportions

When a camera is close to the face, features nearest the lens, such as the nose and lips, look larger, while ears and the sides of the face shrink. This perspective distortion is stronger with the wide lenses on most phones.

A "before" taken at arm's length and an "after" taken a step further away can make a nose look refined without any procedure at all.

**Fix**: fixed floor marks for camera and patient, the same lens setting, no zoom.

## 2. Lighting creates or erases contour

We read the shape of a face from shadow. Overhead light deepens the tear trough, nasolabial folds and marionette lines. Soft frontal light or a ring light fills them in.

So a "before" under an overhead tube light and an "after" under a ring light will show improvement in exactly the areas fillers target, whether or not anything changed.

**Fix**: identical lighting setup at every visit. Our [standardised photo protocol](/blog/standardised-before-after-photos-clinic/) shows a simple two-light arrangement.

## 3. Head tilt changes the jaw and neck

Tilting the chin down by a few degrees compresses the soft tissue under the jaw and makes the jawline look heavier. Tilting it up stretches the neck and sharpens the jaw. This is why some body contouring and jawline "results" are really posture.

Head rotation causes similar problems in oblique views: a few degrees of turn changes how much of the cheek is visible.

**Fix**: level the head using the Frankfort horizontal plane, and use the same rotation reference for oblique views.

## 4. Expression changes everything

A slight smile lifts the cheeks and changes the nasolabial fold. Raised eyebrows smooth the upper eyelid. A tense jaw squares the face. Compare a relaxed "before" with a subtly engaged "after" and you have created a result.

**Fix**: a single, scripted instruction for a neutral expression, used at every visit.

## 5. Makeup, tan and colour settings

Foundation evens tone; a tan hides pigmentation; phone cameras apply automatic beauty filters, HDR and white balance changes. For skin treatments, these can matter more than the treatment itself.

**Fix**: bare skin, beauty modes off, consistent white balance, and ideally a tool that scores skin features consistently, such as the [in-clinic skin analysis](/blog/in-clinic-skin-analysis-explained/) that comes with every Edebo scan.

## 6. Swelling and timing

Filler and surgical swelling can make an early "after" look much bigger than the settled result, setting expectations the final outcome cannot meet.

**Fix**: agreed follow-up intervals per treatment, recorded with each image.

## The deeper problem: 2D cannot measure shape

Even with perfect technique, a 2D photo has no depth. It cannot tell you that a cheek gained 1.2 ml of volume or that chin projection moved forward by 3 mm. It can only suggest change, and the viewer judges it by eye.

That matters for trust. Patients who doubt their result are less likely to return for maintenance. Patients who see a measured change on their own 3D face rarely argue with it. We cover this in [measuring aesthetic results in millimetres](/blog/measuring-aesthetic-results-in-millimetres/) and the retention effect in [aesthetic clinic patient retention](/blog/aesthetic-clinic-patient-retention/).

## What to do instead

- Keep a strict 2D protocol for the views you still need.
- Add **3D scans** for treatments that change shape or volume, aligned between visits so pose and distance stop being variables.
- Show patients the **measured** change, not just two pictures side by side.
- Never edit clinical images, and label any marketing image with the treatment, interval and consent status. See [ethical aesthetic clinic marketing](/blog/ethical-aesthetic-clinic-marketing-3d/).

Honest imaging is not just good ethics. It is good business, because patients stay with clinics they trust.
