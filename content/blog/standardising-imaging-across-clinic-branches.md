For a single clinic, inconsistent imaging is an annoyance. For a chain, it is a strategic problem. A patient treated in one branch and reviewed in another should see a comparable record. Head office should be able to compare outcomes across branches. And the brand promise of "the same standard everywhere" should be visible in the data.

## Why chains drift

- **Different rooms**: lighting, wall colour and space vary.
- **Different devices**: each branch buys its own phones or cameras.
- **Different habits**: one branch shoots five views, another shoots two.
- **Staff turnover**: protocols live in people's heads and leave with them.
- **Scattered storage**: images end up on branch drives, staff phones or messaging apps.

## A chain-wide imaging standard

### 1. One written protocol

Cover room setup, lighting, patient preparation, capture steps, views per treatment, consent and storage. Base it on our [standardised before-and-after protocol](/blog/standardised-before-after-photos-clinic/) and [capture tips](/blog/3d-scan-lighting-and-capture-tips/).

### 2. One room specification

Specify the backdrop, lights, stool and floor marks so every branch can build the same corner. See [clinical photography setup](/blog/clinical-photography-setup-small-clinic/).

### 3. One tool

Use the same capture and storage tool in every branch, with patient records that follow the patient between branches. With phone-based 3D, adding a branch means adding devices and profiles, not installing a rig. Compare options in [3D imaging rig vs smartphone](/blog/3d-imaging-rig-vs-smartphone/).

### 4. Role-based access

Doctors and nurses see their patients; branch managers see their branch; head office sees aggregate data. Every view is logged. This is good practice and supports DPDP Act obligations; see [the DPDP Act and patient photos](/blog/dpdp-act-patient-photos-aesthetic-clinics/).

### 5. One consent form

Use the same consent wording in every branch, with separate options for clinical use and marketing use. See [patient consent for clinical photography](/blog/patient-consent-clinical-photography/).

### 6. Training and certification

Train every staff member who captures images, and sign them off after a supervised set of scans. Retrain when the protocol changes.

### 7. Monthly quality audit

Sample a set of records from each branch every month and score them against the protocol: correct views, lighting, consent recorded, baseline present, review captured.

## KPIs for head office

| KPI | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Baseline completion rate by branch | Shows protocol adherence |
| Review attendance by branch | Shows follow-up discipline |
| Audit score by branch | Shows image quality |
| Conversion and renewal by branch | Links imaging to revenue |
| Consent completion rate | Compliance exposure |

Branches that lead on these numbers usually lead on revenue too. Use them to spread good practice, not just to rank branches.

## Rolling out a new standard

Changing habits across many branches works best in stages:

1. **Pilot** the protocol in one or two branches for a month, and fix what does not work in real rooms.
2. **Appoint an imaging lead** in each branch, usually a senior nurse or coordinator, who owns adherence locally.
3. **Train in waves**, starting with the branches that have the most volume.
4. **Publish branch scores** from the monthly audit internally, framed as improvement, not blame.
5. **Review the protocol** every six months as treatments and tools change.

The imaging lead role matters most. Head office can write a protocol, but only someone in the room every day can make it stick.
